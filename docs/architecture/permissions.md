# Field and record permissions

How a database hides fields and limits records beyond each person's role, and how every read and write honors it.  The rules' shapes live in `packages/contracts/src/data/permissions.ts`, the enforcement in `apps/server/src/data/access.ts` and `apps/server/src/data/permissions/`, and that code is the source of truth where the two disagree.

## The model

- **Roles** stay as they are (`owner`, `creator`, `editor`, `commenter`, `reader`).  Rules only ever take away.  The database's owners always see and change everything, whatever a rule says.
- **Restricted fields.**  A field's `restricted` rule names roles and people.  Only they, and the owners, see the field.  Everyone else never receives its values, its name, or anything computed from it.  "Computed from" is transitive: a formula that reads the field, a lookup or rollup whose target it is, a count or lookup through a link that's hidden, a link whose other side is hidden, a link whose linked table's primary field is hidden (since its names come from that field), a link that follows a hidden field's values, a button whose link fills in a hidden field, and an AI field whose prompt reads a hidden field.  An AI field is also hidden from anyone a record rule limits on a table its prompt reads through a link, lookup, rollup, or count, since its stored value was written through its person's access and could name linked records they can't see.  A field in the trash still counts as something the prompt reads, since what it held is in the stored value, and when what the prompt reads can't be worked out at all, such as a field that's gone or a formula that no longer parses, the AI field is hidden from anyone a record rule limits anywhere in the database.  Once a field the prompt reads is deleted for good, the AI field's stored values are cleared, since their source is gone.  The record's own table needs no such step, since its rules already decide who sees the record.  A field someone can't see reaches their client only as a placeholder (`access.hiddenFields` in the schema: ID, table, and position), which the app shows as "Restricted field".
- **Locked fields.**  A field's `locked` rule names roles and people who may change its values.  Everyone who can see the field still sees it.  Editing a link counts as editing its other side too, so a lock on either side holds for both.
- **Record rules.**  A table's rules each say "the roles and people named here see only records where this condition holds", in the filter model, with `currentPerson` standing for whoever is reading wherever a person's ID goes.  A person named by several rules sees records any of them allows.  Someone no rule names sees every record, and a reader with no role in the database at all, such as a public link with no one behind it, sees none.  A condition that can't be read, such as one on a field that's gone, matches no record, where a view's filter would leave it out and match everything.  Conditions read every value as it is rather than as the reader sees it, so rules never depend on each other.
- **Storage.**  `field_permissions` holds one row per field with a rule, and `record_rules` one row per rule, in order.  Both go with their field, table, or database, and keep while a field or table is in the trash.

## Enforcement: in SQL, from one place

SCIM-managed groups belong to one organization, and group membership uses organization-scoped foreign keys.  Only people the directory provisioned can be members.  Database grants can name a group.  Folder grants also name groups and apply to each database in that folder.  A database belongs to at most one folder.  Editing a folder requires the People administration role and sharing permission on every affected database.  Changing a folder's grants also takes owner access to the folder, through a group it makes an owner, or being the organization's owner, so an empty folder can't carry a role nobody could give.  A database's share list shows the roles its folder gives, read-only and named by the folder.  The same `AccessScope` role lookup resolves direct and inherited grants, checks active organization membership, and chooses the strongest role.  Group roles never reach guests, just as roles shared with everyone don't.  Membership changes send access notifications after commit, so open subscriptions recheck access immediately.  Suspended people keep ownership but cannot use it until restored, and suspension ends their sessions, API tokens, calendar feed addresses, and the provisioning tokens they made for good.  Removing a person's account, whether the directory's delete does it or an owner or People administrator does it for a deactivated managed person in People settings, first gives each database they alone owned, directly or through a group or folder, to the organization's owner, so none is left that nobody can share, trash, or restore.  Scheduled reports offer the people this same lookup lets open the database, through groups and folders too.  Guests see other people's names and addresses only, never their directory records or administrative roles.  An organization import brings its groups and their members along: each imported person joins their groups when they accept the invitation the owner sends from the import review, and the same lookup keeps a group's roles from reaching a guest.

`AccessScope.require` reads the database's rules along with the person's role, once per request.  The synchronous questions then answer from what it read, and asking about a table whose rules weren't read throws rather than guessing.

- `recordPredicate(table, alias)` is the rules' conditions compiled to SQL, qualified by the alias the query gives the table.  Every read path already puts it in its `WHERE`.
- `visibleFields(table, fields)` is the field mask.  The fields it returns also carry, for every link, lookup, rollup, and count among them, the linked table's predicate (`relation.scope`), so `linkedSource` in `physical/relations.ts` reads only linked records the person may see.  Links list those records and count the rest (`RowData.hiddenLinks`), which the app shows as "Hidden record" chips.  Lookups, rollups, counts, and formulas over them compute over the visible records alone.
- Filters and sorts compile against the visible fields, so a condition or sort on a hidden field is left out for that person, and the snapshot cache keeps a separate order for everyone a rule limits (`accessKey`).
- `readsEverything` and `restrictsFields` guard what copies a whole database or leaves the organization: snapshots, restores, duplicates, and templates need every table read in full, templates refuse a database with any rule, and scheduled reports to outside addresses refuse a database with a restricted field.  Copies keep their original's rules.

## Writes

Every change still goes through the command bus, which now checks the rules with the same scope (`permissions/enforce.ts`).  Each refusal has its own message key:

| Change | Refusal |
|---|---|
| A value in a locked field, or in the other side of a locked link | `data.access.fieldLocked` |
| A record the person can't see: update, trash, restore, move, or comment | `data.access.recordHidden` (comments read as not found) |
| A new record, or a change, that leaves the record outside their rules | `data.access.recordOutsideRules` |
| A field they can't see, in a value or a schema change | `data.field.unknown` or not found, as if it didn't exist |
| Settings that use a field they can't see: a view's filter, sorts, or layout, a dashboard, an automation, or an interface | `data.access.fieldHidden` |

A link's list keeps the records the person can't see when they change it, and linking a hidden record is refused.  A linked field limited to a view or by a filter is hidden from anyone who can't see a field the limit reads, and a restore, an undo, or a change to a value the limit compares with doesn't check that field's limit again for them (`WriteGuard.unseen`), since a refusal would name the field and say how its linked records compare.  Settings sent back unchanged keep what's stored, and a view's field order, hidden fields, widths, and summaries keep their entries for hidden fields.  Undo skips what the person can no longer change.  A public form asks nothing about a locked or restricted field.

Only people who can change the schema manage rules, through `PUT /api/fields/:fieldId/permissions` and `PUT /api/tables/:tableId/record-rules`, and only for fields they can see and tables whose records no rule limits for them, so nobody widens their own access.  Each change writes an audit event with IDs only.

## Everything else that reads

- **Public links** read as the person who shared the view when the database has any rule or links to another database, whose own roles and rules then decide what its linked records show, and stop working when that person can't open it.  Since the link reads with the sharer's access, someone a rule limits (`AccessScope.limited`) can't read or change another person's link or where it may be embedded, only stop it, and can't change the settings of a view that another person's link or published site reads.  Nor can they change what such a page's fields hold: adding a field to a table whose link shares every field its view shows, restoring a field, changing a field's type or options, changing the primary field, undoing any of those, or changing a named function is refused when the page shows the field, shows anything worked out from it however many steps away (`AccessScope.computedFrom`), or follows a view shaped by either (`data/commands/public-pages.ts`).  Renaming and trashing stay open, since neither shows anything more.  What was there before a view was made public is what its sharer shared, so nothing looks back at earlier changes.  A link whose view's filter holds a condition that can never apply, or one on a field its sharer can't see, closes rather than widening.  Whatever the rules, a link answers 410 Gone while the person who shared it is suspended.  Its share settings show it paused and name that person, and it works again when they're reactivated, or when someone else who may share the view shares it again, which keeps the address and makes them the link's sharer.  Removing their account stops their links for good.  Public forms follow the same rule: paused while their sharer is suspended, taken over by sharing again, and stopped for good on removal.  A field restricted from anyone never goes out, and a view that filters, sorts, colors, sums, or lays out by one stops working until it doesn't.  **Published sites** follow the same rule through the same code (`publicAccess` and `viewShapedBy` in `data/shared-view-reads.ts`), and read as their publisher whatever the rules: a field restricted from anyone, or computed from one, never goes out in a block, record page, address filter or sort, number, chart, or image, even when the publisher can see it, and a block, number, or chart whose source, view, grouping, or value names one shows nothing until it doesn't.  A site stops when its publisher can't open the database or leaves, and answers 410 Gone while their public links are turned off (`apps/server/src/sites/`).
- **Public forms** add each response as no one, with the form's own questions and rules, so a response belongs to no person and nobody can undo it.  That's the same whether or not the form takes payment.
- **Payment result fields** (status, amount paid, and reference) are marked `managed` in their stored definition, and only the payment service writes them.  The physical writers refuse any value for a managed field unless a grant names that exact field in that exact record, and only the payment service makes grants: for the Pending values of the record a form or booking page adds, and for what a verified Stripe webhook reports, as a system change with no person's rules.  A managed field's definition can't change, a replace through the API leaves it alone, an automation step can't name it, merges and undo skip it, and an organization import leaves its values empty (`apps/server/src/data/managed.ts`, `apps/server/src/payments/`).
- **Booking pages** read and write as the person who last saved their settings, through their scope: a time is open when fewer of the records they can see overlap it than it has places, each booking adds a record as they would, rules and all, and the page stops taking bookings once they can't add records to the table.  Visitors only ever learn whether a time is open (`apps/server/src/bookings/`).
- **Signature requests** read and complete as the requester, through their current record and field permissions.  A source attachment restricted from anyone cannot be sent for signing, and a status or completion date field the requester can't see or change is refused with the same `signatures.invalidField` as one that's missing or of the wrong type.  Listing a record's requests needs edit access to the record, so readers and commenters see none.  Public responses contain only the recipient's fields, where the other signers' fields sit, the document, the other signers' names, and whether they have signed, and every refusal on a public link, the requester losing access included, is the same 404 `signatures.unavailable`.  Public links lock only the request's own row, never the database.  Completion attaches the scanned PDF and updates selected fields through the command bus as the requester, from the signature worker (`apps/server/src/signatures/`).
- **Calendar feeds** read as the person who made them, through their scope: their record rules and the view's filter pick the events, fields hidden from them never reach an event's notes, and the address answers 410 Gone once they can't open the view.  Suspending the person stops their addresses for good, as it does their sessions and API tokens (`apps/server/src/calendar-feeds/`).
- **AI fields** run as the person who last saved their settings, through their scope: the prompt may name only fields they can see, each run reads its record through their rules and writes its value through the command bus as them, and runs stop with `aiFields.owner.lostAccess`, `aiFields.owner.fieldHidden`, or `aiFields.owner.recordsLimited` once they can't.  Someone a record rule limits on a table the prompt reads through a link can't save its settings (`apps/server/src/ai-fields/`).
- **Routines** read and write as their owner, through their access at each step, and pause once their owner can't open the database or has left.  Routines change records only, never on their own, and never write a signature.  Every change a run proposes, only in the tables its plan names, waits for its owner, and applies through the owner's access at the time they approve, acting as the owner's own change would, once the server works it out again and finds it's what they were shown.  A run reads, and emails its owner, and does nothing else unattended.  An owner who can no longer open the database loses what their routine left waiting.  Pages are read only at addresses in the owner's words or at the roots of sites the owner approved.  An admin with the AI role sees a routine's words, plan, and recipients only in a database they can open themselves (`apps/server/src/routines/`).
- **Webhooks** hear only what the person who made them could see, so someone a field or record rule limits can change, or replace the secret of, only the ones they made (`webhooks.rulesLimited`).  **Mentions** notify only people who can see the record.  **Automation runs** read and write as their person, and their details go only to people no rule limits.  **Automation tests**, whole or single-step, read and write as the person testing.  **Automation simulations** read and work out their changes as the person simulating, never as the automation's person, and say when that person's access reads differently.  Their conditions and a trigger's filter compile against the visible fields, so a condition on a hidden field is left out and shown as hidden, and a lookup or count reads only the linked records that person may see.  **Diagnose** gives Rio a failed run's values only when no field or record rule limits the person asking anywhere in the database, since a run reads as its person and even a value they can see, such as a link's names or a count, can come from records they can't.  Anyone else's model gets the automation's settings with their literal values held back, the errors, and the names and types of the fields they can see.  For everyone, a webhook step's address reaches the model only up to its path, its headers by name, and its body with literal values held back (`apps/server/src/ai/diagnose.ts`).  **Script panel runs** read and write as the person running them, rules and all, and a script's code goes only to people who can change the schema.
- **Files** open only through a field the person can see, in a record they can see, or when they uploaded the file and it isn't in a record yet.
- **SQL access** reads through the person's scope: each table a query names becomes a subquery of their visible fields and records, and a column reference that doesn't resolve to one of them is refused as unknown (`apps/server/src/sql/`).  The outside login reads as one person an admin chooses.  An admin whose own access a rule limits may choose only themselves, so nobody widens their own access, and a rebuild turns the login off with an audit event once the chosen person's access grows past that admin's.  SQL access can reveal roughly how many records a table holds through query timing, so admins should give it only to roles that may know that.

## Realtime: filtered per connection

Each subscription keeps its person's scope.  For someone no rule limits, changes go out as they are.  For anyone else, the hub rewrites each change for them (`realtime/redact.ts`): records they can't see are left out, record values are read again through their scope (so hidden fields never go out, links count hidden records, and lookups compute over visible ones), comments on hidden records are dropped, and settings lose the values of hidden fields.  A schema change that moves what they may see reloads their tab, and presence leaves out records and cells they can't see.

A rule change is recorded in the database's change log as `access.changed`.  The hub never sends it: it reloads every tab of the database at that point in the order, so nothing after a rule change goes out under the old rules.  Tabs also recheck on the access notice and whenever the listener reconnects.

## OAuth app grants

OAuth access tokens authenticate through `apps/server/src/authorization/store.ts` and produce the same scoped actor as personal access tokens.  A person's consent for an app, its scope set and either all visible databases or a chosen list, is one record that every installation of the app shares.  Each authorization is an installation grant of its own, bound to one exact resource, with its own refresh tokens.  A token may do what its installation asked for, as far as the consent still allows, and a refresh may narrow that further for its access token.  The consent is read on every request, so approving again with fewer scopes or databases narrows every installation at once.  The API and MCP reject tokens for any other resource.  Existing `AccessScope`, field masks, record predicates, and command checks apply unchanged.  Browser-session routes and the compatibility API do not accept these credentials.  Approving an app takes the same fresh second step as making a personal access token.

Migration `0051_oauth_apps` stores app registrations, consents, installation grants, consent requests, hashed credentials, and the webhooks each installation made.  Database row locks serialize code consumption, refresh rotation, app revocation, and grant revocation, taken in the order person, membership, client, consent, and grant.  Person and membership locks prevent issuance racing suspension.  Used credentials remain available for reuse detection, which revokes that installation in a transaction that commits before returning the refusal.  Revoking a live token ends only its installation, and revoking a connection ends them all.  Database triggers permanently revoke consents and grants on suspension, person deletion, membership removal, and conversion to a guest, and provisioning revokes them itself when it suspends or removes someone.  A trigger's audit event credits whoever the transaction names in the `riosoma.acting` setting, or no one, and names the app.  However a grant ends, a trigger turns off the webhooks it made and holds their deliveries.  Every registration, authorization decision, token issue, refresh, reuse detection, expiration, and revocation is audited without credentials.


## Organizational units

Migration `0057_org_units` makes unit references organization-scoped, clearing any that crossed into another organization, and adds unit admin grants (`org_unit_admins`), provisioning mappings (`scim_unit_mappings`), the level that set each unit value (`org_units.policy_levels`), and how each session was proven (`sessions.second_step_verified`, `sessions.sso_verified`, and `sessions.sso_provider_id`).  `apps/server/src/org-units/` is the one place unit authority and policy are decided: `store.ts` for who reaches what, `policy.ts` for inherited policy, and `scim.ts` for placement by provisioning.  A tree has at most eight levels.  Unit changes, placements, provisioning, and turning identity providers off take turns on one lock per organization (`lockUnits`), and every change checks the admin's reach again after taking it.

**Authority.**  Organization admin roles and unit admin grants are separate.  A unit grant's roles reach the unit and every unit below it, read fresh from the database on each check.  A unit role reaches a person only when they're placed inside it, never the owner or anyone with an organization admin role, and never another admin unless each unit they administer sits strictly below the admin's own grant.  It reaches a database or folder only when every person who owns it, directly, through a group, through a folder, or as everyone in the organization, is someone it reaches, and a source only when the role reaches the person who made it and every database it fills.  Filing a database in a folder takes the folder it leaves as well as the one it joins.  A unit role never opens a database: `AccessScope` is unchanged, so a unit admin reads and shares only what's shared with them, as any member does, and sees the same member directory any member sees.  Changing a unit itself, or who administers it, takes a grant on a unit above it, and a grant can give or take away only the roles its admin holds there.  Deleting a unit takes, above it, every role held by admins inside it and the standing to remove each of its values, and moving one takes the standing to remove the values it leaves behind.  Neither goes ahead unless the admin reaches everyone placed in the unit or below it.  The owner is never placed in a unit and needs no grants.

**Every admin route declares a scope** (`http/policy.ts`): `organization`, `subtree`, `person`, `database`, or `source`.  A route without one, with one the server doesn't know, with a policy kind it doesn't know, or under `/api/admin/` without an admin policy, stops the server from starting, wherever the route is mounted.  At run time a policy the server doesn't know admits nobody.  `organization` routes refuse anyone without the organization role.  The other scopes let a unit admin with the role through, and the middleware then checks the `:userId`, `:databaseId`, `:sourceId`, or `:unitId` the route names before its handler runs, refusing any it can't read, such as one over 100 characters, rather than skipping the check.  `subtree` handlers check each target and filter each list themselves.  `org-units/units.test.ts` walks every admin route as a unit admin holding every role, against outside targets that all exist (a unit, a person, their database, source, group, app connection, and a folder they own with the admin), and requires 403 or a list with nothing from outside.  `org-units/walk-mutations.test.ts` switches each reviewed handler's own unit checks off and shows the walk then fails.

**Policy.**  Each setting in `UNIT_POLICY_RULES` names its direction, the admin role that changes it, and where the organization's own value lives.  The organization's values come from the settings screens that already own them, tightened by its unit policy (`orgs.settings.unitPolicy`), which also lists the settings units may override.  Each unit in the person's chain may then change a listed setting, security settings only in the stricter direction.  Each unit value records the level that set it: the organization, or the highest unit the admin held the setting's role for.  Only an admin at that level or above changes or removes it, and a save leaves out, and so keeps, whatever was set from above.  A delete or move that leaves a recorded level outside a unit's chain re-levels that unit's values to the nearest unit of its old chain above the level that remains, or to the unit itself when none does, never to the organization, so nobody new is bound and nobody who could change a value loses it.  The audit entry lists each (`releveled`), and a stored level that isn't in the chain anyway reads as the unit's own.  A value applies only to the people its level reaches: the organization's reach everyone but the owner, and a unit's reach only the people that unit's admins could act on directly, so never an organization admin or an admin of that unit or one above.  An override the organization no longer lists, or that has become looser than what's above it, stays stored and is skipped, so a later tightening always wins.  Sessions last at least a day and may sit idle at least an hour, and the owner is held only to the server's own session limits, never to single sign-on or a password length.  Guests are never held to single sign-on.  Single sign-on can't be required without an identity provider turned on, the last one can't be turned off or deleted while it's required, and when none is on anyway the requirement waits with a warning.

The effective policy is worked out from the database, once per request: the session resolver works it out and keeps it on the actor for every later check, and an organization with no units and no unit policy reads only its own settings.  Checks of what a person may do (`permissions`, behind `policyAllows` and `requirePolicy`) read an organization with no units in one query of its own settings, and public links, forms, booking pages, and calendar feeds read whether the organization has units or a unit policy in their own lookup (`policyRelevant`), so without either they cost no more than before units.  The resolver ends a session older or idler than the person's policy allows, or one whose second step or single sign-on the policy requires and that no longer holds: a second step the person has since removed, or a provider or link since removed or turned off.  Personal access tokens and apps' tokens are refused while their person's policy requires a second step or single sign-on they haven't set up.  The organization's own second-step policy still applies at each sign-in only.  Everything that read an organization-wide switch now reads the effective policy of the person behind the action: sign-in, sessions, tokens, theme and level choices, public links, forms, and booking pages, calendar feeds, guest invitations and sharing with guests, scheduled reports to outside addresses, signature requests, text messages from automations, feedback publishing, and AI.  A public link whose sharer loses public links answers 410 Gone, as a suspended sharer's does.  The session's `org.settings` are always the organization's own, which only the organization's editors show and save, and `effectiveSettings` carries what applies to the person.

**Audit.**  Unit changes, placements, admin grants, unit values with their levels, the organization's unit policy, and provisioning mappings each write an entry with before and after values, and the units they concern (`unitId`, `unitIds`).  Admin actions on a person record the person's unit.  A unit admin reads only entries naming a unit they reach.  Entries naming no unit are for organization admins alone.

**Provisioning** places people by the department of their enterprise record, the one enterprise attribute Riosoma stores, so a placement always follows its latest value.  Division, organization, and cost center are ignored whatever their type, as before units, and a PUT or PATCH that turns `active` off suspends the person even when the rest of it is refused.  Once a mapping exists, it decides the placement of everyone it manages, on every provisioning write and at once when mappings change, and admins can't place those people by hand.  A value nobody mapped places the person in the organization itself and never refuses the write, so suspension and removal always go through.  Only organization admins with the Security and People roles delete a unit a mapping names.

**Archives** carry the tree, its values and the levels that set them, the organization's unit policy with its settings, and each person's unit and unit grants in their manifest entry.  Imported people get them when they accept the invitation the owner sends from the import review.  Organization admin roles never come back through an archive.
